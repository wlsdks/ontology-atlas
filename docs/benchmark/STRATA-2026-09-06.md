# Strata — the techniques it is built from, and the ones it refused

The 2026-09-06 decision record (`docs/DECISIONS.md`) names these in one line
each; it has 2,000 bytes and no room for the reasons. This page carries them.
It is a **sources page, not a plan**: the geometry lives in
`buildStrataTargets` (`src/widgets/topology-map-v2/model/dome-view.ts`) and the
numbers live in `tests/e2e/map-3d-strata-drawing.spec.ts`.

The rule for everything below: **ideas were taken, code was not.** Nothing here
was copied from another project, so no licence follows any of it into this
repository. Where a licence is worth knowing anyway, it is stated.

## What the classic literature decided for us

| Source | What it settled |
|---|---|
| Sugiyama, Tagawa & Toda, "Methods for Visual Understanding of Hierarchical System Structures", IEEE SMC 11(2), 1981 | The five-phase frame: assign layers, then reduce crossings by ordering *within* a layer, then assign coordinates. Strata's three passes are phases 2, 3 and 4 of exactly that. |
| Bachmaier, "A Radial Adaptation of the Sugiyama Framework for Visualizing Hierarchical Information", IEEE TVCG 13(3), 2007 | How to run that frame when the layers are concentric circles instead of lines: the ordering key becomes circular, and the layer is read as a cut of the circle. The paper reports ~30% fewer crossings than the horizontal form, mostly because a radial edge may go round the centre either way. |
| Gansner, Koutsofios, North & Vo, "A Technique for Drawing Directed Graphs", IEEE TSE 19(3), 1993 (`dot`) | Keep the **best** ordering the sweeps produce, not the last one. Measured here before the guard existed: 16 sweeps drew *more* crossings than none (2,600 against 2,520). |
| Reingold & Tilford (1981); Buchheim, Jünger & Leipert (2002), as radialised by d3-hierarchy | A child inherits an angular sector from its parent, sized by subtree weight. This is the property that makes "this capability sits under that domain" a glance. |
| Robertson, Mackinlay & Card, "Cone Trees", CHI 1991 | What Atlas already ships as the Cone. |
| Cockburn & McKenzie, "An Evaluation of Cone Trees", BCS HCI 2000 | Why a second arrangement was worth building at all: subjects located data **slower** in a cone tree than in an ordinary tree browser, and got worse as the branching factor rose, even while saying they liked it. Enthusiasm is not legibility. |
| Carriere & Kazman, "Interacting with huge hierarchies: beyond cone trees", 1995; the Helix Cone Tree | Occlusion is the cone's structural cost, and the fix is to stretch the ring the children sit on. Strata's two alternating radii per plane are that idea in the one dimension a flat stratum has left. |
| Lamping, Rao & Pirolli, hyperbolic browser, CHI 1995; Munzner's H3 | Rejected. Focus+context in hyperbolic space is a second navigation model for one view, and Atlas already has ego focus and realms. |

## What people are actually shipping and writing, 2025–2026

This is the half the papers cannot give: what practitioners chose, and what
readers complained about.

- **`vasturiano/3d-force-graph` and `force-graph`** (MIT, actively released
  through 2025). Its `dagMode` accepts `'radialout' | 'radialin' | 'td' | 'bu' |
  'lr' | 'rl' | 'zin' | 'zout'`, with `dagLevelDistance` for the gap between
  depths. Two of its API decisions are the same answers Strata reached
  independently, and finding them shipped is the reason they are worth keeping:
  `dagNodeFilter` leaves an excluded node "unconstrained and free to move"
  rather than forcing it into a level it does not belong to — Strata's rim; and
  `onDagError` treats a cycle as a case to survive rather than to throw on —
  Strata cuts a containment cycle at the first repeat. `radialout` itself is a
  *radial force* pulling depth `d` toward radius `d`, not a sector layout, so
  siblings there are free to slide past each other; Strata keeps the sector.
- **`d3-hierarchy`'s `cluster`** (ISC). Its own documentation is the clearest
  statement of what Strata does: a cluster layout "places leaf nodes of the tree
  at the same depth", and "you can treat `x` as an angle and `y` as a radius to
  produce a radial layout". Strata is that, with one change: the level is the
  node's **kind**, not its depth in the tree, so an element directly under a
  domain still lands on the element plane. In Sugiyama's terms the layer
  assignment is *prescribed* rather than computed, which is why phase 1 of the
  framework does not appear in the code at all.
- **Cytoscape.js layout guidance** (`cytoscape.js-elk`, `cytoscape.js-fcose`,
  both actively maintained). The practitioner split is exactly the one Atlas now
  offers: **ELK `layered`** for a graph that is a DAG with clear dependency
  flow, and **fCoSE**, a *compound* spring embedder, for a graph whose
  containment is drawn as nesting. Cone is the nesting answer and Strata is the
  layered one, and the honest reading of that literature is that neither
  dominates — which is why this ships as a third arrangement rather than as a
  replacement.
- **yFiles' hierarchical-layout notes and NDepend's architecture posts** make
  the same case for tiers that Atlas's own domain/capability/element spine
  makes: a layered picture is chosen because "items depend on the items below
  them" is the fact a reader is looking for.
- **The Obsidian graph-view criticism of 2025–2026** is the sharpest warning,
  and it is about our category, not our algorithm. The recurring complaints:
  the force-directed view is "beautiful and almost completely useless", it turns
  into a hairball past a few hundred notes, and it shows no status, staleness or
  filter — "a diagnostic, not a brain". Strata does not answer the second half
  of that (Atlas answers it with ego focus, the datasheet and the index), but it
  does answer the first: a plane and its name are a **typed** reading that a
  force layout cannot give, and the reader gets it without touching anything.

## What was deliberately not adopted

- **Free layer permutation.** Ordering a whole plane without regard to parentage
  finds fewer crossings and destroys the one reading the arrangement exists for.
  The sweep permutes a node only among its own siblings.
- **Edge bundling.** It reduces crossings by making individual relations
  unfollowable, and "which relation is this" is a question Atlas must answer.
- **Dummy vertices for long edges.** The textbook framework splits an edge that
  spans several layers so every segment is between neighbours. Atlas draws the
  relation itself, and a reader clicking a bundle of invented waypoints would be
  clicking things that are not in their vault.
- **three.js.** The probe's own recommendation. Its report, THREE-PROBE-2026-09-06,
  is on the `feat/three-probe` branch beside the probe it describes; nothing on
  `main` depends on it, which is why it is named here rather than cited as a path.
